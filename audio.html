Max Palat tells his story

Max Motel Palatnik Palat (1902–1990), recorded telling his granddaughter about his travels from Europe to America.

Palatnik lineSon of Pincus (Philip) Palat and Sarah (Sura) SokolovHusband of Jennie Shandel GoldbergBorn in Teplyk, 20 March 1902

Max was the youngest of seven children and grew up in Teplyk, a town he remembers as about 5,000 people, with the Jewish families living in the middle and most of the merchants Jewish. He recalls five synagogues, and says the Jews and their farming neighbours on the outskirts "got along with them really well, until the war broke out." He describes spending time at his oldest brother's carpentry and glass shop near the family home, and a household of eight people sharing one bedroom, sleeping on the floor.

Side A runs about 47 minutes and Side B about 24 minutes. Each side has its own player below.

Interview summary and transcript

Side A: Growing up in Teplyk (approx. 47 minutes)

"I am the youngest of seven children. I grew up in a small town of 5,000 called Teplyk in Ukraine. Such towns were usually situated such that the Jewish people lived in the center of the town — most merchants were Jewish and the town contained five synagogues — while the gentiles and non-Jews were in the outskirts, farmers. We got along with them really well, until the war broke out."

"As for his own day-to-day life, he frequented his oldest brother's carpentry and glass shop near his home. He also describes his home: 8 people, 1 bedroom. They slept on the floor."

Side B: Continued (approx. 24 minutes)

The second side continues with Max's journey to America. His daughter Elaine provides additional details in her companion interview (JHS 35):

  • Max left Teplyk at age 19, saying "Times are bad, I'm leaving"
  • The journey took 2 years
  • He arrived at Ellis Island in 1923
  • He settled in Trenton, New Jersey
  • He and his wife Jennie (formerly a seamstress in Philadelphia) opened Fries Bakery on Union Street in partnership with Sam Clempert
  • Max was active in Trenton's Jewish community, joining the Jewish Community Center and YMCA

Source: JHS 37, "Palat, Max," on the Internet Archive (Trentoniana collection, Jewish Historical Society oral histories, Trenton, New Jersey).

Max and Herman's journey from Teplyk to America

A best-guess route drawn from Max's own account (JHS 37). Solid markers are places he names; dashed legs and "?" stops are inferred. He says he was about 19 when he ran, so probably 1921, and gives his arrival as 29 October 1922. (Elaine's interview says 1923.)

The map shows the European leg. From Danzig the Polonia took 13 days to New York.

  1. Teplyk, Ukraine. Max leaves in the chaos of the civil war, with Kerensky money and a basket of food.
  2. Bershad (best guess). By horse and wagon to a town where a cousin lived, with a ferry crossing on the way. A guard searches their baskets for hidden money; a $5 bribe settles it. The recording is unclear; the name is my guess.
  3. Kamenka (best guess), on the Dniester, then the Soviet-Romanian border. First attempt to cross at midnight: caught by border guards and jailed overnight, then released. Could also be Yampol or Kamenets-Podolsk.
  4. Second crossing of the Dniester. The guide delivers them to bandits who rob them. They walk to Soroca (best guess), the first Bessarabian town, and knock on the one house with Friday-night candles. Town name is my guess.
  5. Kishinev (Chisinau), Bessarabia, then Romania. Aid from HIAS, a soup kitchen, a job, and and in the border town Herman turns up from the jail.
  6. Bucharest. Waiting for affidavits from Uncle Hymie; the consul; the paper with a number. The Celia meeting is at the HIAS mail board; city not certain.
  7. Through Germany to Danzig (Gdansk). Disinfection and about five days in quarantine. Route and port are my best reading of the audio.
  8. The ship Polonia (Red Star Line), 13 days at sea, very seasick, then the Statue of Liberty.
  9. New York, 29 October 1922. Then Trenton, New Jersey.

The surprises and turning points

Under the bed

Sick with typhoid, Max is left alone as soldiers ransack the house, and hides under the bed, trying not to breathe. A soldier picks up a bag of his mother's silverware and leaves without looking under it.

Side A, about 23:00–26:00

The thirteen-year-old "manager"

When the First World War takes his oldest brother and his workers, 13-year-old Max runs the shop for his brother's wife and six children. And that wife is also his half-sister: "my father's daughter married my mother's son."

Side A, about 9:00–11:00

"Why should you go to America?"

A talented brother had papers to emigrate, but their father talked him out of it. He went to the war and was killed.

Side A, about 12:00

Caught, jailed, and let go

At the Dniester the border guards jail Max and about 25 others. The officer scoffs, "You want to go to America? We'll show you America," and the judge warns that next time they will be thrown in the water. They laugh and try again.

Side A, about 35:00–38:00

Friday-night candles

Robbed by bandits and wandering lost in the dark, Max sees one house with lit candles, speaks Yiddish at the door, and is taken in by a Jewish family.

Side A, about 41:00–43:00

Meeting Herman

In the refugee town, Max is told someone in the jail says he is his brother. It is Herman, who had followed him: unshaven, dirty, in a peasant's coat. Max pays a guard to get him out, takes him to the bathhouse, and buys him a coat. From then on they travel together.

Side A, about 44:00–46:00; Side B, start

Meeting Celia

Reading the HIAS mail board, Max sees a letter for a name he recognizes from the family: Celia, a cousin from Odessa whom he had never met. They wait at the office for her to come, and she turns up with her child.

Side B, about 10:00–12:00

Argentina or America?

Waiting for papers, Max writes to an aunt in Argentina, who sends tickets and money at once. But the tickets from America arrive too, and they choose America.

Side B, about 14:00

Sweet-talking the consul

Told their quota number is six months away, Max skips the three-block line, tells the consul they are "two poor boys" and asks for an earlier number. The consul says to come back in a week, and when they do, their papers are ready.

Side B, about 16:00–19:30

Elaine (Palat) Lavine remembers her parents

Recorded on 4 May 1996 for the Jewish Historical Society's oral history project in Trenton.

Palatnik lineDaughter of Max Palat and Jennie Shandel GoldbergGranddaughter of Pincus (Philip) Palat

Elaine is asked to recall her childhood and her time with her parents, and tells the story of her father, Max Palat. Born in 1902, he came to the United States in 1923 through Ellis Island. He left Russia at 19, with the words "Times are bad, I'm leaving," and then spent two years trying to reach America. In Trenton he enjoyed the Jewish community and joined the Jewish Community Center, the Y.

Her mother had come from Poland and worked as a seamstress in Philadelphia. Together her parents bought a bakery, Fries Bakery on Union Street, in partnership with a Sam Clempert. It is a companion to Max's own account above.

This is Side A, about 47 minutes. The archive's description says the interview continues on a Side B, which is not online.

Interview summary and transcript

Elaine is asked to recall her childhood and her time with her parents, and tells the story of her father, Max Palat. Born in 1902, he came to the United States in 1923 through Ellis Island. Two sisters from Teplyk, on video

Sof'ia Palatnikova (1927–2021) and Tatiana Marinina (1921–2010) were interviewed in Teplyk by the Archive of Historical Eastern Yiddish Memories (AHEYM) at Indiana University. They are daughters of Moshe Palatnik and granddaughters of Pincus (Philip) Palat.

Palatnik lineDaughters of Moshe Palatnik and Rakhil' KonstantinovnaGranddaughters of Pincus (Philip) Palat

Sof'ia Palatnikova: "as soon as they attacked, they were already here"

Sof'ia describes how her family tried to leave Teplyk before the Germans arrived on 26 July 1941, but waited too long, and the Germans made them go back. She goes on to describe the Teplyk ghetto. There is more of her story, and the written summary, on the Shoah Victims page.

Tatiana Marinina: Seder on a kolkhoz

Tatiana lived through much of the 1930s on the Lunacharskii Jewish collective farm in Crimea with her sister Sof'ia. Here she remembers how Passover brought the whole farm together. During the war she survived the camps at Bershad and Raygorod.
