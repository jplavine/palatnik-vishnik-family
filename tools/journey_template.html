Max & Herman's Journey

In about 1921, Max Palat, then 19, left Teplyk in the chaos of the civil war and made his way to America, with his brother Herman, who joined him on the way. Every stop below is taken from Max's oral history. Press a ▶ button to hear that part of the recording.

Max Palat's oral history (JHS 37)

  • Side A begins at about 0:12, when Max starts speaking. The transcript's first line, at 0:17, is part of his opening dedication to his granddaughter Sharon.
  • Side B starts in the middle of the story, with Max meeting Herman in the Romanian refugee town, and ends with Max's last words, about 24 minutes in.
  • Times shown on this site are approximate, to within about 10 seconds.

Max and Herman's journey from Teplyk to America

A best-guess route drawn from Max's own account (JHS 37). Solid markers are places he names; dashed legs and "?" stops are inferred. He says he was about 19 when he ran, so probably 1921, and gives his arrival as 29 October 1922. (Elaine's interview says 1923.)

The map shows the European leg. From Danzig the Polonia took 13 days to New York.

  1. Teplyk, Ukraine. Max leaves in the chaos of the civil war, with Kerensky money and a basket of food.
  2. Bershad (best guess). By horse and wagon to a town where a cousin lived, with a ferry crossing on the way. A guard searches their baskets for hidden money; a $5 bribe settles it. The recording is unclear; the name is my guess.
  3. Kamenka (best guess), on the Dniester, then the Soviet-Romanian border. First attempt to cross at midnight: caught by border guards and jailed overnight, then released. Could also be Yampol or Kamenets-Podolsk.
  4. Second crossing of the Dniester. The guide delivers them to bandits who rob them. They walk to Soroca (best guess), the first Bessarabian town, and knock on the one house with Friday-night candles. Town name is my guess.
  5. Kishinev (Chisinau), Bessarabia, then Romania. Aid from HIAS, a soup kitchen, a job, and and in the border town Herman turns up from the jail.
  6. A rabbi's group pass. In Kishinev, Max says a rabbi of great authority (probably Chief Rabbi Yehuda Leib Tsirelson) got the officials to issue a group certificate so the refugees could travel to Bucharest. The identification is my inference from the name and role.
  7. Bucharest. Waiting for affidavits from Uncle Hymie; the consul; the paper with a number. The Celia meeting is at the HIAS mail board; city not certain.
  8. Through Germany to Danzig (Gdansk). Disinfection and about five days in quarantine. Route and port are my best reading of the audio.
  9. The ship Polonia (Red Star Line), 13 days at sea, very seasick, then the Statue of Liberty.
  10. New York, 29 October 1922. Then Trenton, New Jersey.

The surprises and turning points

Under the bed

Sick with typhoid, Max is left alone as soldiers ransack the house, and hides under the bed, trying not to breathe. A soldier picks up a bag of his mother's silverware and leaves without looking under it.

The thirteen-year-old "manager"

When the First World War takes his oldest brother and his workers, 13-year-old Max runs the shop for his brother's wife and six children. And that wife is also his half-sister: "my father's daughter married my mother's son."

"Why should you go to America?"

A talented brother had papers to emigrate, but their father talked him out of it. He went to the war and was killed.

Caught, jailed, and let go

At the Dniester the border guards jail Max and about 25 others. The officer scoffs, "You want to go to America? We'll show you America," and the judge warns that next time they will be thrown in the water. They laugh and try again.

Friday-night candles

Robbed by bandits and wandering lost in the dark, Max sees one house with lit candles, speaks Yiddish at the door, and is taken in by a Jewish family.

Meeting Herman

In the refugee town, Max is told someone in the jail says he is his brother. It is Herman, who had followed him: unshaven, dirty, in a peasant's coat. Max pays a guard to get him out, takes him to the bathhouse, and buys him a coat. From then on they travel together.

Meeting Celia

Reading the HIAS mail board, Max sees a letter for a name he recognizes from the family: Celia, a cousin from Odessa whom he had never met. They wait at the office for her to come, and she turns up with her child.

The rabbi who got them out

Without travel papers the refugees could not leave Bessarabia. Max says a rabbi with great authority spoke to the governor, and a group certificate let them go to Bucharest. This was probably Yehuda Leib Tsirelson, Chief Rabbi of Bessarabia and then a member of the Romanian parliament.

Argentina or America?

Waiting for papers, Max writes to an aunt in Argentina, who sends tickets and money at once. But the tickets from America arrive too, and they choose America.

Sweet-talking the consul

Told their quota number is six months away, Max skips the three-block line, tells the consul they are "two poor boys" and asks for an earlier number. The consul says to come back in a week, and when they do, their papers are ready.
