Where the family came from · Vinnytsia region, Ukraine

Teplyk & Zhabokrych

The oldest Palatniks were born in Zhabokrych. Moses Aaron (Moshko) Palatnik's children were born from the 1840s in Teplyk, about 65 km (40 miles) to the east, while his brother Berko's children and grandchildren were born in Zhabokrych into the 1870s. Sarah (Sura) Sokolov, both of her husbands and Yankel Meir Vishnik's children were born in Teplyk. Both towns had large Jewish communities until the Holocaust.

Teplyk · Teplik

Teplyk district, Vinnytsia region · the family's main hometown

Jews settled in Teplyk in the early 1700s, and by 1765 there were 289. The community suffered in the Haidamak uprisings of the 18th century but grew to become a majority of the town. In the 1897 census, 3,725 of Teplyk's 7,044 residents were Jewish.

About 400 Jews were killed in a pogrom in 1919. Under Soviet rule the town had a Jewish council, artisan cooperatives and, from 1929, a Jewish elementary school with 262 pupils. By 1939 the Jewish population was down to 1,233.

German forces occupied Teplyk on 26 July 1941 and set up a ghetto and a Judenrat. Young people were sent to a forced-labor camp. On 27 May 1942, 769 Jews of Teplyk were shot, together with 520 Jews deported there from Bukovina. A small group of skilled workers kept alive for their labor was killed later.

Zhabokrych · Zhabokrich

Kryzhopil district, Vinnytsia region · birthplace of the oldest Palatniks

Zhabokrych is first mentioned in 1559. Jews settled there in the first half of the 1700s, with 6 families in 1765. Haidamak attacks reduced their numbers in the 1770s, but by 1790 there were 55 Jews. The town passed to the Russian Empire in 1793. The community grew to 665 people by 1847, with a wooden synagogue recorded in 1853, and by 1889 there were two synagogues and four chadarim (religious schools). In 1897, 1,307 of the town's 6,252 residents were Jewish. They worked as shoemakers, furriers, carpenters, blacksmiths and other craftsmen.

Pogroms in 1917 and 1919 took many lives. In the 1920s there was a Jewish soviet and a Jewish orphanage, and a Yiddish school opened briefly. The famine of 1932–33 struck Jews and their neighbors alike. Many younger people left for the cities, and a Jewish collective farm was set up near the town.

German and Romanian forces occupied Zhabokrych in July 1941. On 27–29 July, Jews were murdered at three sites in the town; a few of those shot were only wounded and escaped. From September the town was part of Romanian-ruled Transnistria. About 245 Jews deported from northern Bukovina and Bessarabia were housed in the homes of the murdered, alongside about 200 local survivors, in a ghetto marked by hunger, disease and forced labor.

  • Location: north-western edge of the village, beside 147 Zabolotny Street (48.3951, 28.97864)
